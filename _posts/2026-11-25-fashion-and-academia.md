Being a college professor is appealing for several reasons, but one of them is the freedom it allows in so many aspects of the job, including in how you dress.

I’ve been reading and (lightly) engaging in the menswear community for over a decade – putting increasing energy (and money) into the hobby over the years. I often see people online lamenting that no one ever sees and appreciates the effort they put into their fits because they work in a corporate environment or, even worse, work from home. So, this post is a short reflection on how being a college professor is possibly the very best job for people into fashion.

## An established cultural aesthetic

Even those not into fashion have a strong understanding of the cultural aesthetic of the academy and university professors. This primarily comes from popular media – there are many examples of academics with incredible fits: from Robin Williams in *Dead Poets Society* to Scott Cohen in *Gilmore Girls* (yeah, maybe Max wasn’t right for Lorelai but he put that shit on). See more examples [here](https://bamfstyle.com/tag/professor/).

In normal white collar/corporate jobs, dressing in a collegiate/professorial style would likely be seen as inappropriate (and probably derided for being out of the norm). In my personal opinion, the corporate expectations for how you should dress are hopelessly boring. By contrast, the classic academic style is so rich and textured. Take tweed: rumor suggests it became the professorial uniform because it was warm and nearly indestructible in drafty old campus buildings, and the elbow patches (borrowed from shooting jackets) were there to absorb years of leaning on a desk. Either way, the look was established early – in 1938, [Esquire](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2010/09/big-scale-tweeds-and-the-right-cap.html) called the patched tweed jacket “the latest trick at the smart Eastern universities.”

I sometimes read fashion advice that boils down to – dress for the lifestyle you actually live. If you are a hiker, wear sick techwear/outdoor gear. If you work a physical labor job, wear heritage workwear. The nice thing about being an actual professor is that it gives you permission to really lean into the cultural aesthetic that we assign to the job – tweed sportscoats, corduroy, etc. Though by my observation, it is rarely taken advantage of!

## Captive audience

It is hard to motivate yourself to dress well when there is no audience to appreciate it (see this incredible article [advocating for tracksuits during COVID quarantine](https://www.insidehook.com/style/red-adidas-tracksuit-wfh-uniform)). Teaching provides a truly captive audience (at least if they come to class). For me, that means 80-100 students are forced to bask in the glory of whatever struck my fancy that morning. To be sure, 95% of them don’t care whatsoever, and maybe a few think I look bad, but I tend to have a couple that let me know they like my fit.

## Flexibility and a low bar

Professors have a huge amount of leeway in how they perform their job, a concept called academic freedom. This is mostly cited as why faculty should be allowed to research their chosen topics and determine the content of their courses. This principle also (somewhat) extends to the way that faculty dress. Business schools tend to be a bit more conservative/professional than other colleges, but I’ve never been aware of any stringent policies on dress at any university I have been.

Like in all areas of society, the average man doesn’t really care about fashion or style, resulting in a relatively low bar to be seen as well-dressed. This is even more true for college professors; the stereotype of the tenured faculty member who exclusively wears cargo shorts and Hawaiian shirts exists for a reason (and to be clear, I think it is great).

## Limited days and the academic calendar

As much as I enjoy getting a fit off, I’m not sure I would want to do so a full five days a week for a normal working year. A massive benefit of the professor schedule is teaching only 2-3 days a week, creating a natural weekly cycle of days worth putting the additional effort in, mixed with chill days in the office where I don’t have to care quite as much. In addition, the academic calendar provides a long summer break – helpful especially because summer is, for me, the toughest season for dressing well (and in the heat of Mississippi I quickly devolve into rugby shorts and light sweaters to handle the AC).

## Insight into the future

As fashion trends are primarily driven by young people, seeing what college students are wearing every day is a fun perk of the job. And while collegiate style has changed over time (mostly in the ever more casual direction), remnants of the collegiate style I am so fond of persist (a vintage sweater from your college can never fail you).
